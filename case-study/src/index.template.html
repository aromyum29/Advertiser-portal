Turning Koko's unused app screens into a self‑serve ad product merchants actually want to buy

Koko is a buy‑now‑pay‑later app with 2.5M installs and 600K monthly shoppers. Its home screen, search results and post‑checkout page were valuable real estate nobody was selling. I designed the product that sells it: fixed‑price, first‑come‑first‑served ad space that a merchant can book in under five minutes, then built and tested it as a working prototype.

The problem

Two problems, one product

Koko needed a revenue stream that did not depend on transaction fees. Merchants wanted more sales from the 600K people already shopping in the app, but had no way to be seen beyond their own store page.

My role

End to end, alone

I ran the research, wrote the business logic with Koko's ops and finance leads, designed every screen, and built a typed, tested prototype the engineering team can lift the rules from directly.

What changed

A bookable inventory

Six ad spaces became calendar inventory with fixed prices. A merchant picks a space, picks dates, and pays. No bidding, no sales call, no ad‑tech vocabulary. Six of six testers completed a booking unaided.

01

Context

Koko had an audience it wasn't monetising and merchants who couldn't reach it.

Koko is a BNPL platform in Sri Lanka. Shoppers pay in instalments at partner stores, in person and in the app. The app itself had grown into a shopping destination: a home screen with featured stores, a trending row, search, and a post‑checkout page every buyer sees.

Those screens were curated by hand. A marketing team decided who got the hero banner, usually whoever asked loudest or had the biggest account. Nobody paid for it. Meanwhile Koko's revenue was almost entirely merchant transaction fees, which put a ceiling on growth and made every margin negotiation painful.

The brief from leadership was blunt. Sell the space. Make it something a merchant can buy without a sales team in the middle. Keep prices fixed, because an auction would scare off the long tail of small merchants who make up most of the platform.

The merchant side was less obvious. Merchants already had a portal for orders, QR payments and finance. Advertising was a new idea to most of them. Whatever I designed had to earn a place in a portal they used to run their business, not sit there as a marketing upsell they scrolled past.

The constraint that shaped everything: fixed prices, first come first served, and a buyer who has never bought an ad before.
For Koko

A second revenue line, priced on inventory rather than negotiated per account. Ops sets the rate card; the product sells it.

For merchants

A way to reach shoppers who already have credit approved and are looking for somewhere to spend it.

02

Research

Merchants didn't want ads. They wanted to know if Koko was sending them customers, and they wanted certainty about what they'd pay.

I interviewed 20 merchants over two weeks: five key accounts (multi‑store retailers with a marketing team) and fifteen long‑tail merchants (owner‑operated, one or two locations). Each conversation ran 40 to 50 minutes and covered how they used Koko today, what they knew about their Koko customers, and how they'd react to paying for visibility.

I split the sample deliberately. Key accounts are where the money is per booking, but the long tail is where the volume is, and the two groups wanted different things. Key accounts asked about guaranteed placement during festive weeks and whether they could lock out competitors. Long‑tail merchants asked whether they'd understand what they were paying for, and whether they'd need to hire a designer.

The most consistent finding wasn't about advertising at all. Seventeen of twenty could not say how many of their customers came through Koko versus walked in off the street. They saw the settlement report and nothing else. That gap turned into the performance surface, and into the decision to lead every screen with views and store visits rather than price.

The second finding decided the pricing model. Twelve merchants, unprompted, said some version of "I don't want to bid." Three had tried Facebook or Google ads and stopped because they couldn't predict the spend. Fixed prices weren't just a leadership constraint; they were the thing that made the product credible to the people buying it.

03

Framing the problem

Design a booking flow, not an ad platform. The mental model is a hotel calendar, not an auction.

After research, I wrote the problem as a single sentence and pinned it above the work: A merchant who has never bought an ad should be able to buy the right space for the right week and know exactly what they'll pay and roughly what they'll get, in one sitting, without talking to anyone at Koko.

That sentence ruled out most of what ad products usually do. No campaign objectives, no audiences, no bid strategies. It also set the shape of the work: inventory that can be shown on a calendar, prices that can be added up by hand, and estimates that come from real traffic numbers rather than promises.

I wrote the business rules down with Koko's ops lead and finance before drawing a screen. Base price per space per day, position multipliers, weekend and campaign uplifts, and the discount stack. That document became the contract between design, engineering and finance. Every rule in the final product traces back to a line in it.

Constraints I designed inside
  • Fixed prices, first come first served. No auctions.
  • Inventory and prices are set by ops, not by the product.
  • Creative review takes up to two business days, so bookings need lead time.
  • Plain language. No ad‑tech vocabulary, no dashes in merchant copy.
  • Bookings are final. That has to be unmistakable before payment.
04

Four rules I held myself to

Each one came out of the interviews. Each one is visible on every screen.

1

Value before price

Estimated views render above the price on every card, and the landing page hides prices by default. Seventeen merchants didn't know what Koko sent them. Show that first, then what it costs.

2

One decision at a time

Spaces, then dates, then positions, then artwork, then payment. One accordion open at a time. The bundle offer only appears after the first space is fully decided.

3

Honest persuasion

Every "most popular" badge, multiple and estimate is wired to a real number or marked as a placeholder for one. One badge per decision, at most. Scarcity comes from the calendar, not from copy.

4

The total never surprises

Rs. 0.00 until dates exist. The design fee appears only when the artwork step is reached. Payment fees appear only on the payment step. The number moves for reasons the merchant has just seen.

The product, step by step

Each step below is a working simulation of the real interaction, followed by the reasoning. Try them.

7.1 · Choose where you want to be seen

Live simulation

1
The screenshot is the explanation.Every card shows a real Koko screen with the bookable zone outlined. "Where does my ad go?" was the first question in every interview. The highlight is always visible, not revealed on hover, because nobody should discover the answer by accident.
2
Views lead, prices follow.The number on the card is estimated store visits over seven days. Prices are behind a toggle. The long tail compared on reach and stopped anchoring on the hero's price.
3
Multi‑select with a floating summary.One booking can cover several spaces on the same dates, which is how the bundle discount becomes possible later. The bar is the only pill‑shaped element in the product; it's meant to feel separate from the page.

7.2 · Pick your dates

1
Campaign weeks first, own dates second.The two paths are a radio choice at the top. Campaign weeks carry a traffic multiple and a 15% saving for taking the whole period; they're the highest‑margin thing Koko sells and the thing key accounts asked for by name.
2
Every day carries its own price.What a Tuesday costs depends on whether it's inside Diwali, a weekend, or neither. Burying that in a total would make the calendar feel arbitrary. Blocked days say why: "Lead time" or "Booked".
3
One upsell at a time, and only when it's bookable.Under seven days you're offered a week at 5% off. Eight to twenty‑one days, a month at 10%. The offer is suppressed if the extended range would cross a booked day, because an offer that fails when accepted is worse than no offer.

1
One function prices everything.The summary rail, the review table, the confirmation dialog and the stored booking all read from the same quote(). In the first prototype they each did their own arithmetic and two of them disagreed.
2
Progressive reveal is a pricing rule, not a UI trick.The function takes a "stage" argument. Design fee is withheld until Assets is reached; payment adjustment until Payment. The rail can't leak a fee the merchant hasn't been shown.

7.6 · Confirm, and what happens next

1
A second confirmation, on purpose.Bookings are final and non‑refundable. "Confirm and pay" opens a recap instead of paying. The finality notice appears three times on the way in: in the body, in the rail, and in this dialog. Nobody in testing called it excessive.
2
The thank‑you page answers "now what".Reference, amount, and a plain sentence about what happens to the artwork: near‑instant for Koko‑designed, up to two business days for uploads. Then a direct route to My bookings, where the status will change.
3
The run ends with a recap, not a grey row.When a booking finishes, the merchant gets a shareable summary: store visits, orders, cost per order, sales against spend. The primary button is "Book this space again". This is the retention mechanic.

7.7 · Closing the loop

This page exists because of the first research finding. Seventeen merchants couldn't say what Koko sent them. Store visits, attributed orders, order value and ad spend on one chart, by day, week, month or year, with each booking's own results one click away.

"Attributed" is defined on the page in one sentence. A merchant who doesn't trust the number won't book again, and a merchant who doesn't understand it can't trust it.

08

Validation

Six moderated sessions, two rounds of changes. Every tester finished a booking; the changes came from where they hesitated.

I ran moderated sessions with six merchants from the interview pool, four long‑tail and two key accounts, on the clickable prototype. The task was open: "Book advertising for the week you'd most want to be seen." I watched for hesitation, back‑tracking and questions, and timed the run from landing to confirmation.

Completion isn't the interesting number in a six‑person study. The interesting part is where people slowed down. Three testers stalled on the positions step with the original sticky total in view, and two of them picked the cheapest slide rather than the one they'd said they wanted. That's what moved the summary under the content. Two testers missed the campaign weeks entirely in the first round because the flow started with positions; that's what moved dates to the front.

The finality notice was the thing I was most nervous about. Three repetitions felt heavy on the page. Not one tester mentioned it, and two said afterwards they'd noticed and were glad. It stayed.

09

Business impact

A rate card nobody was selling becomes roughly Rs. 10M a month of sellable inventory. The design's job is sell‑through.

The inventory model is simple enough to do on a napkin, which is deliberate. Six spaces, each with a number of sellable positions and a base price per day. At the rate card we set with finance, the full inventory is worth about Rs. 332,000 a day, or just under Rs. 10 million a month if every slot sold at base price with no campaign uplift.

Nobody expects full sell‑through. What matters is that the number scales with the design decisions in this case study rather than with headcount. Campaign weeks multiply the day rate by 1.4× to 2.7×. Hero slide 1 carries a 35% premium. The bundle offer adds a second space to a booking for a 5% discount, which is worth it when the second space would otherwise sit empty.

Against the alternative, a sales‑led ad business, the model has no per‑booking human cost. That's what makes the long tail addressable at all. A Rs. 19,500 post‑checkout booking doesn't justify a sales call. It justifies a good form.

The build

I built the prototype to production standards so the rules could be lifted into the real system, not redrawn from screenshots.

Business rules as code

One pure pricing engine

Inventory, campaigns, availability, creative spec and pricing live in a typed domain layer with no UI in it. The same module can run on the client to quote and on the server to charge.

Tested where money is

128 unit tests

Rounding per space per day, campaign multiples beating the weekend uplift, the order savings stack in, progressive fee reveal, lead time and occupancy, spec boundaries, and every draft interaction rule.

Accessible by audit

Zero axe violations

WCAG 2.1 AA across all eight pages. Keyboard‑only sign‑in and booking, skip link, reduced motion respected everywhere including the confetti.

Real behaviour

State that persists

Bookings you make appear in My bookings and survive a reload. A half‑finished draft survives a reload too. The prototype behaves like a product, not a slideshow.

Honest about what it is

Placeholders marked

Engagement ladder, campaign multiples, "9 of 10 merchants": each is named in code as a stand‑in for the benchmarks service. Nothing simulated is presented as measured.

Handoff

Architecture doc with a swap table

A table of what's mocked today and which service replaces it. The pricing engine was written as pure functions over plain data specifically so it could cross that boundary unchanged.

11

What I'd tell myself at the start

Write the rules before the screens. Order beats polish. Test the thing you're nervous about.

The rules document was the best week I spent

Writing the pricing model with finance before any UI meant every later argument was about a line in a document, not a mockup. It also made the prototype possible: there was one truth to build against.

Sequence changed more than any visual did

Dates before positions. Total below content. One campaign at a time. None of those show up in a screenshot, and each one moved behaviour in testing more than any card design.

I underestimated occupancy

The prototype simulates booked days at the account level. Real inventory is booked per space and per position. The calendar and the upsell logic both need that, and it should have been in the rules document from day one.

Six testers is enough to find order problems

It's not enough for anything statistical, and I've been careful not to claim otherwise. Both structural changes came from watching two or three people hesitate in the same place.

Building it changed the design

Two pricing inconsistencies and an upsell that could book over a taken day only surfaced because the prototype ran real rules. A Figma prototype would have shipped all three.

Next

Per‑position occupancy. A waitlist that actually notifies. An ops surface to set the rate card. And the real numbers behind every badge, once the benchmarks service exists.
